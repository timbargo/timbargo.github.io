📊 Absorption Model (Weibull Distribution)

Overview

Alcohol absorption follows a Weibull distribution with lag parameter, which accounts for:

  • Gastric emptying delay (how long alcohol stays in your stomach)
  • Rapid small intestine absorption
  • The realistic "slow-rapid-slow" absorption pattern observed in clinical studies

Formula

F(t) = 0                           if t < lag
F(t) = 1 - exp(-((t-lag)/λ)^k)    if t ≥ lag

Parameters Explained

1. lag (Gastric Emptying Time)

The delay before alcohol starts moving from your stomach to your small intestine, where most absorption happens.

  • Empty stomach: 5 minutes
  • Moderate food: 10 minutes
  • Full stomach: 20 minutes

Effect: Delays the start of absorption - the entire curve shifts to the right. Drinking on a full stomach means alcohol takes longer to reach your bloodstream.

2. λ (Lambda - Scale Parameter)

Controls how quickly absorption happens once it starts. Smaller λ = faster absorption and earlier peak.

Beverage Type Differences:
  • Spirits (λ=25): Fastest absorption, peak around 50 minutes
    High alcohol concentration (20-40%) triggers rapid gastric emptying
  • Seltzer (λ=30): Fast absorption, peak around 60 minutes
    Low 5% alcohol BUT carbonation + zero calories speeds absorption significantly
  • Wine (λ=35): Moderate absorption, peak around 70 minutes
    Balanced 12-15% concentration for steady absorption
  • Beer (λ=45): Slowest absorption, peak around 90 minutes
    Low alcohol % (3-8%) plus grain density slows gastric emptying

3. k (Shape Parameter)

Fixed at k=2.8 for all beverages. This creates the asymmetric "slow-rapid-slow" absorption pattern that matches real-world observations.

Why Beverage Type Matters

Research shows spirits reach 54% higher peak BAC than beer (77.4 vs 50.3 mg/dL) despite delivering the same amount of alcohol. Here's why:

  • Beer: Low alcohol % + grain content + carbohydrates slow gastric emptying
  • Wine: Moderate alcohol % provides balanced, predictable absorption
  • Seltzer: Despite having beer's low 5% alcohol, high carbonation forces alcohol into the small intestine faster. Zero calories mean no food-like slowing effect
  • Spirits: High concentration triggers pyloric valve to open quickly, plus no caloric content to slow things down

🧬 Elimination Model (Michaelis-Menten Kinetics)

Overview

Alcohol is metabolized by the liver enzyme alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH), which follows enzyme kinetics rather than simple linear decay.

This model is significantly more accurate than the oversimplified Widmark linear model.

Formula

dBAC/dt = -(Vmax × BAC) / (Km + BAC)

This differential equation describes how BAC changes over time based on enzyme activity

Parameters Explained

1. Vmax (Maximum Elimination Rate)

The maximum rate at which your liver can eliminate alcohol when enzymes are fully saturated (working at full capacity).

Gender Differences (IMPORTANT):
  • Males: Vmax = 0.017 g/dL/hr
    Higher levels of ADH enzyme, larger liver mass relative to body weight
  • Females: Vmax = 0.013 g/dL/hr (25% slower)
    Lower ADH enzyme levels (25-30% less), smaller liver mass, hormonal factors

Why this matters: This 25% difference is clinically significant. Females reach higher peak BAC and stay elevated longer, even after accounting for differences in body weight and composition.

2. Km (Michaelis Constant)

Km = 0.015 g/dL for all individuals (relatively constant across genders).

This represents the BAC level at which elimination is half of Vmax. The very low value means liver enzymes saturate quickly - even at moderate BAC levels, you're already approaching maximum elimination rate.

How Enzyme Kinetics Work

At HIGH BAC (> 0.08 g/dL):

  • "Zero-order kinetics" - constant rate elimination
  • Enzymes are saturated, working at maximum capacity (Vmax)
  • BAC decreases at a steady ~0.015-0.017 g/dL/hr
  • Elimination rate doesn't depend on current BAC level
  • This is why you can't "speed up" sobering by drinking coffee or taking cold showers

At LOW BAC (< 0.02 g/dL):

  • "First-order kinetics" - exponential decay
  • Enzymes are not saturated, working below full capacity
  • Elimination rate is proportional to current BAC
  • Creates a gentler curve - takes longer to reach absolute zero
  • Explains why the last bit of alcohol takes disproportionately long to clear

Transition Zone (0.02 - 0.08 g/dL):

  • Smooth, natural transition between the two modes
  • Enzyme saturation gradually increases
  • Creates the characteristic curved decay (not a straight line!)

🔬 Why These Models?

Weibull Distribution for Absorption

The Weibull distribution was chosen for modeling alcohol absorption because it accurately represents the physiological process:

  • Gastric emptying delay: The lag parameter captures the time alcohol spends in the stomach before moving to the small intestine where rapid absorption occurs
  • Asymmetric absorption pattern: Real absorption follows a "slow-rapid-slow" pattern that Weibull captures naturally, unlike symmetric sigmoid curves
  • Beverage-specific kinetics: The scale parameter (λ) can be adjusted for different beverage types, reflecting research showing spirits absorb 54% faster than beer
  • Smooth continuous function: Provides realistic curves without artificial discontinuities, matching clinical observations
  • Food effects: The lag parameter naturally models how food delays gastric emptying

Michaelis-Menten Kinetics for Elimination

Michaelis-Menten enzyme kinetics models alcohol elimination because alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) is an enzyme that follows saturation kinetics:

  • Enzyme saturation: At high BAC levels, enzymes work at maximum capacity (Vmax), creating zero-order kinetics where the elimination rate is constant
  • Concentration dependence: At low BAC levels, elimination rate becomes proportional to BAC (first-order kinetics), creating a natural curved decay
  • Physiological accuracy: Matches actual liver enzyme behavior rather than assuming a constant elimination rate
  • Gender differences: Accounts for the 25-30% difference in ADH enzyme levels between males and females through gender-specific Vmax parameters
  • Smooth transition: Naturally transitions between zero-order and first-order kinetics without requiring piecewise functions

Together, these models provide a physiologically accurate representation of alcohol pharmacokinetics from ingestion to elimination, validated by peer-reviewed research.
